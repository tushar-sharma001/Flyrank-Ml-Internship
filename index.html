01 Abstract

Out of a large content inventory, only a small fraction of pages can realistically be reviewed by a content team in any given week. This work builds a ranked scoring system that answers a narrow, practical question: out of thousands of pages with real search visibility, which ones should a reviewer look at first?

The system was built on FlyRank's pseudonymized search & engagement warehouse, under the Refresh / Content Opportunity Scoring lane. A transparent, rule-based baseline was compared against Logistic Regression and Random Forest, validated on a client-grouped holdout split so that the model could not simply learn to recognize which client a page belonged to.

The project's most important result isn't the final AUC number — it's what happened on the way there. An early version of the decline label produced a 98.2% positive rate, which on inspection turned out to be mostly day-to-day noise rather than genuine decline. Rebuilding the label around a smoothed future window and a real magnitude threshold brought the positive rate down to a defensible 37%, and only then did the model comparison become meaningful. Under the corrected label, Random Forest reached an AUC of 0.937 against a rule baseline of 0.626 — a large, genuine gap. A specific, named failure mode (the model under-flags recently updated pages with zero clicks) is reported openly below, and shapes the final recommendations rather than being hidden from them.

This is decision-support for a human reviewer, not a guarantee about any individual page, and not evidence about how Google's ranking algorithm works.

02 Introduction & Problem Statement

A large content portfolio accumulates pages faster than any team can manually track them. Some are genuinely losing visibility and worth a refresh; many more just look concerning at a glance and aren't. The cost of getting this wrong runs in both directions, and both directions are real:

FALSE POSITIVE

A fine page gets flagged. The cost is wasted reviewer time — a real cost when review capacity is the scarce resource.

FALSE NEGATIVE

A genuinely declining page is missed. The cost compounds silently until the loss is much larger and harder to recover.

Because reviewer time is the binding constraint, this project optimizes for precision at the top of a ranked list rather than recall across the whole inventory — the goal is not to find every possible issue, it's to make sure the first fifty pages a reviewer opens are worth their time.

What was deliberately excluded, and why

  • Product-decision fields (health_score, priority_score, action_type) — not shipped in the release on purpose, and using them would mean the model learns to copy FlyRank's existing rules rather than discover anything of its own.
  • Raw query, URL, and title text — scrambled before release; never reconstructed or requested.
  • Pre-tracking GA4 rows — zero-filled placeholders before a client's ga4_data_start, which is not the same thing as real zero engagement, and was excluded rather than silently treated as a true zero.

04 Methodology

Unit of analysis

One (client, content item) pair, with features aggregated over the 90 days prior to a decision point.

The label — and the bug that was caught and fixed

The first version of the decline label compared a single day's impressions against the same page's impressions exactly 30 days later. This produced a 98.2% positive rate — almost every page in the dataset was technically "declining." That's not a plausible real-world rate, and it turned out to be a real methodological bug, not a real finding: single-day impression counts are naturally noisy, so comparing one day to one other day mostly measures that noise, not a genuine trend.

Caught during validation, not beforeThis surfaced specifically during the Week-6 validation audit, where the exercise of auditing a published research paper's methodology was turned back on this project's own model — exactly the intended lesson.

The corrected label compares an 11-day smoothed future window (days 25–35 out) against the prior-90-day daily average, and only counts it as a real decline if the drop exceeds 10% and the page had at least 10 average daily impressions to decline from in the first place — a real magnitude threshold and a real volume floor, not just "lower than before." This brought the positive rate to a defensible 37%.

Features

All aggregated strictly over the prior-90-day window, so nothing here could have been known only after the decision point: impressions_prior90, clicks_prior90, avg_position, ctr_prior90, days_since_update.

Baseline

A fully transparent rule, stale_but_visible: flag a page if days_since_update ≥ 180 AND impressions_prior90 ≥ 500. Every model in this study is measured against this rule on the identical test split, not against an abstract "no model" baseline.

Validation design

An 80/20 client-grouped split — whole clients held out, never split across train and test. Pages from the same client likely share structural patterns (site health, publishing cadence, template), and a naive random row split risks letting the model "cheat" by recognizing a client it already saw in training rather than learning a portable signal.

Before/after check on the split design itselfA direct comparison against a naive random split (under the original label) showed nearly identical AUC — 0.774 naive vs. 0.773 grouped. That's a reassuring result: it means the model wasn't secretly inflating its score by recognizing clients, so the extra rigor of grouping wasn't hiding a problem this time. Worth checking regardless of the outcome.

Leakage audit

Three checks, every time: zero client overlap between train and test (verified explicitly with a set intersection, not assumed); zero product-decision or label-derived columns present in the feature list; and a feature-importance sanity check against a 0.7 threshold.

One flag worth disclosing, not hidingUnder the corrected label, impressions_prior90 carries 71.3% of Random Forest's feature importance — above that 0.7 threshold. Investigated and judged not to be literal leakage (the feature is legitimately prior-window, never touches the future), but a real, named confound: the label compares each page against its own prior average, so high-volume pages mechanically have more room to show a 10%+ drop, and may regress toward a lower baseline independent of any real, actionable trend.

All three ranking methods clearly beat random selection on precision@50 (0.860 vs. 0.370) — genuine top-of-list value exists before comparing the methods to each other. The three methods tie exactly at precision@50, which at first glance looks uninteresting — but AUC reveals a large, meaningful separation the top-50 metric can't see: Random Forest (0.937) substantially outperforms Logistic Regression (0.755) and the rule baseline (0.626) at ranking the entire candidate pool correctly. Notably, days_since_update — the single strongest signal under the earlier, noisier label — is nearly irrelevant here. That's a meaningful finding in its own right: the old result was likely an artifact of label noise, not a durable staleness signal.

Error analysis

The three worst errors are all false negatives — real declines the model was roughly 99% confident would not happen. All three share a specific, repeatable profile: real impression volume (200–232), zero clicks, and exactly 34 days since the page was last updated. The model appears to read "recently updated" as an implicit safety signal, even when zero engagement over that same window is arguably the stronger warning sign. This is a concrete, named failure mode — not a vague "the model is sometimes wrong" caveat — and it directly shapes Priority 1 in the recommendations below.

06 Limitations & Honest Framing

  • CTR-blindness on visible-but-unclicked pages — per the error analysis, the model under-weights zero-CTR pages that still carry real impressions. A future iteration should add an explicit interaction feature (high impressions AND near-zero clicks) rather than relying on the model to discover that combination unassisted.
  • Volume-magnitude confound — impressions_prior90's dominant importance (71.3%) may partly reflect that higher-volume pages mechanically have more room to show a 10%+ drop against their own baseline. Worth testing a relative, percentile-based volume feature in a future iteration.
  • Observational, not causal — a page being flagged does not mean refreshing it will cause recovery. Proving that requires a controlled experiment this dataset alone cannot provide.
  • Single-month development slice — validated on one mid-panel month; the sealed final month remains a true held-out test, not yet reported on here.
  • Unbalanced client history — some clients have 12+ months of tracking, others only weeks. A 90-day feature window isn't available equally across the inventory, which likely over-represents longer-tracked clients in the ranked output.
  • No composite scores used as inputs — health-score-style fields were never used as a feature or a label, only referenced as background context where explicitly noted.

07 Ranked Recommendations

A three-tier review priority, built directly from what the error analysis actually found — not a generic best-practices list.

Priority 1 — High impressions, zero clicks, position outside top 20

This is the model's specific, named blind spot. Review these manually even when the model's own score is low, since the error analysis shows the model systematically under-flags exactly this pattern.

Priority 2 — Top Random Forest scores, confirmed by the rule baseline

Where the model and the transparent stale_but_visible rule agree, confidence is highest — two independently-built signals pointing the same direction.

Priority 3 — High Random Forest score, rule baseline disagrees

Worth a second look specifically to understand why the simple rule missed it — often a page that's visible and CTR-weak but not yet 180 days stale, a pattern the rule can't see but the model can.
